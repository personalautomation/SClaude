Why Postgres

One database for both transactional and analytical load, deep tuning, access rules down to the row (RLS), and point-in-time history out of its write-ahead log. A PoC will not outgrow it.

The main pain · AI that does not stay in line

Prompts are not guarantees. An orchestrator is.

Even with explicit instructions and plenty of IMPORTANT warnings, a model will invent a new status, switch language or skip a predefined option. A production workflow needs more than good intentions.

Plan→ Code→ Pull request→ AI review→ QA→ Merge→ Production
Fewer tokens

Context, models, scripts

Each stage is a fresh session with only its context. Cheaper models where the top one is not needed. Deploys, tests and bookkeeping run as scripts, at zero tokens.

Control

Tools are banned, not discouraged

Each step gets only the tools it needs; merging, main and production are refused as they happen. AI cannot drift out of control, so neither can the work.

Predictability

One fixed path

The same stages, gates and hand-offs on every feature. A step closes on evidence, never on a model saying "done".

Scale

No load on the team

One copy for everyone, updated on their next command. The process runs itself; nobody polices it in meetings.

Also for the CTO: a sandbox per feature, so a mistake stays small (next slide); one record per feature, so DORA metrics come from real cycles; rules the team improves from evidence. In the industry's words: spec-driven development with Docker's 3Cs as the safety model.

The third pain · know-how that walks out of the door

Process, prompts and lessons become company capital

Today each developer keeps their own prompts, workarounds and tricks, and the tools change faster than anyone writes a tutorial. Here all of it lives in git, where it compounds instead of walking out of the door.

What is kept
  • The process: stages, gates, prompts
  • The rules: what each step may and may not do
  • The friction: every refusal, reviewed and decided
  • The lessons: retrospectives and the rules they produced
How it spreads
  • A fix reaches every developer at their next command
  • A personal rule that proves itself becomes the team's
  • A new hire starts with everything the team has learned; sclaude doctor sets up the machine
How it teaches
  • Each retrospective tells the developer, from their own run, how to work better
  • "What went well" surfaces best practices, with evidence
  • Whoever lags gets coached from facts, not in a meeting

Know-how in practice · token secrets

Six habits that cut the token bill

The kind of know-how that used to live in one person's head. Most of it is built into the flow; the rest is shared as team practice.

1 · The cache lives an hour

A subscription keeps the prompt cache for an hour (five minutes on credits or an API key). Warm, the context is re-read at a tenth of the price or less; cold, it is paid in full. Take on no more parallel features than you can answer within the hour.

team habit
2 · A fresh session per step

Stale context is paid for on every message. Starting clean costs nothing; compacting a long conversation is itself a large request.

built in
3 · The right model per step

Opus where judgement matters, Sonnet for the pull request and the write-up, no model at all for deploys and gates.

built in
4 · Scripts before tokens

Deterministic work runs as scripts. A hook can cut a ten-thousand-line log to its failures before the model reads it.

built in
5 · Lean always-on context

CLI tools rather than MCP servers; workflow instructions as skills that load on demand; CLAUDE.md under 200 lines.

team habit
6 · Delegate the noisy parts

Sub-agents keep long outputs out of the main context. Agent teams cost about seven times a single session.

built in

The second pain · parallel features

Three features at once, and none of them can break another

With an agent per feature, a developer runs several features in parallel. In one shared checkout they collide: one migration wipes another's data, signing in to one feature signs you out of the next, two dev servers fight over a port.

Its own code

A worktree

A separate copy of the repository on its own branch. Edits never meet until the pull request.

Its own address

feature.localhost

A dev server per feature behind one address each, so cookies and sign-ins stay apart too.

Its own database

Supabase in Docker

Filled from the main local database or the seed, removed with the feature. A migration reaches nothing else.

Its own preview

Vercel per branch

QA checks each feature at its own deployed address, never on a shared staging server.

The channel · where development actually runs

In a startup, the laptop is the server

The orchestrator runs the work on the laptop. The phone is only a channel to it, and the laptop was never built to be reached.

Corporations

VMs, virtual desktops, VPS

Always on and reachable, with an IT team behind them. That costs money every month, and a two-person PoC rarely gets one.

Startups

Personal or company laptops

Windows 11 with WSL2, or macOS with Apple Container instead of a VM. Free and fast, but it was never designed to be reached from outside.

Which leaves three problems to solve, on the next slide

1 · Reaching it

The dev environment sits behind the laptop, inside WSL or a container. Its address is not reachable from a phone. Usually solved with Tailscale or similar.

2 · Keeping it awake

A closed laptop sleeps and remote work stops. Keeping it awake all the time wears out the battery and costs power.

3 · Using it from 6 inches

An SSH client on top of a VPN works, but a terminal on a phone screen is slow for anything routine.

The channel · layers 1 and 2

The Claude app covers the conversation. The full kit covers the rest.

A desk session shows up in the Claude mobile app, with remote control always allowed. Everything else on the laptop needs a way in.

Layer 1 · Claude app does well
  • Continue the session the laptop started
  • Read the plan, correct the scope, approve it
  • Answer the agent's questions and permission prompts
  • Give the next instruction on the way to a meeting
Layer 1 · it cannot reach
  • A command line on the machine
  • Our orchestrator and its questions
  • The feature on its test server
  • Test tools, logs, a quick edit
  • Anything unusual on the way
Layer 2 · the full kit
  • Tailscale: one private network; SSH listens only there, never on a café's Wi-Fi
  • Termius: SSH with a key made on the phone; sclaude, full-screen Claude, any command
  • code-server: VS Code in the phone's browser, started on demand

The kit is good for long hands-on stretches, anything stuck or non-standard. It is the spare key that always works.

Too heavy for "does the page look right?", "restart the server", "yes, the step is done": seconds of intent, minutes of setup, three apps and a VPN.

What I found in the team

Every developer had built the same bicycle

I looked at the helper tooling each developer had made for themselves. Nearly everyone had spent time and tokens on the same few things, each in their own way.

Rebuilt most often

Switching to the phone

Every AI client can mirror a desktop session to mobile. It is not enough: people also need a shell, the orchestrator, test servers, test tools, a basic editor, whatever the moment calls for.

Rebuilt quietly

Keeping the laptop alive

A sleeping laptop ends remote work. A laptop that never sleeps burns power and battery. Everyone found a different workaround.

Rebuilt expensively

AI code review

The know-how of AI development now sits in review. Each person tuned their own prompts and settled on a different trade-off between missed bugs and wasted tokens.

A company can provide this once, centrally, and let everyone contribute back.

That is what this stack and its tooling are: the common flow, found across individual setups, then automated and shared. First stop: a Telegram client for the 80%.

Keeping the laptop available

keepawake: awake while there is work, asleep when there is none

A person is there

An SSH session, an open editor tab or traffic over Tailscale holds the laptop awake for as long as it lasts.

Work with nobody watching

A busy agent or a running dev server holds it an hour at a time. Thirty minutes before the end the bot asks whether to extend.

Probably forgotten

An hour held with no sign of anybody, and the bot asks whether a session was left open.

When in doubt, it lets go. An early sleep costs one tap. A laptop that never sleeps costs a battery.

Why it matters for the budget

It lets a six-year-old laptop do the job a paid VPS would, three features in parallel included. Electricity and battery wear are the only running costs, and keepawake keeps both down.
