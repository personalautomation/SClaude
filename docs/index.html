The problem

Give a team an AI agent, and in a few months it is no longer one team.

A few people are ahead of the whole planet; others barely keep up. The leaders have no time to teach — a developer's job is to develop, not to mentor — and each of them spends about half their time, and their tokens, building the same private tooling, falling into holes a neighbour climbed out of long ago. A zoo of bicycles.

Those who do build a harness usually build it out of the agent itself — a crew of AI agents, a set of skills, a hierarchy of markdown instructions. What they rarely know is that such a construction does not repeat and does not scale: instructions are advice, not guarantees, so even with every IMPORTANT in capitals a model invents a status, skips a predefined option, and over time the process drifts. And it eats tokens on work a script would do for free.

€200a month on AI subscriptions for some developers — while others used a quarter of a €100 plan. The leaders did not need a bigger plan. They knew how the model thinks.
~50%of the AI champions' time and tokens, in the team we looked at, went into tooling — each building the same set, independently.
×Nevery developer fell into the same holes, because nobody shared what they learned.

SClaude fixes both: the process moved into code, the tools moved into the company's git, and the model does only what it does better than a script: planning, writing and reviewing code.

The problem

Excited by the speed of AI, few people have the energy left to review its work and check its decisions. We trust AI more and more, and that is the new reality: it gets more and more access — and for some teams it even deploys to production.

SClaude's answer

SClaude does not just trust the agent. Every step closes on a result, not on a model saying "done"; the merge, main and production are closed to it, refused the moment it tries; and the review arrives prepared, so a person decides in minutes.

How it works

One feature. One fixed path. Every time.

The agent cannot skip a step or invent a new one. Spec-driven development (Augment Code) with an agent doing the typing. For each step SClaude starts a fresh session with its own model, permissions and exit — so nothing gets lost in a conversation that is too long, and you do not pay for old context.

Acceptance criteria are a gate, not a request. Step 1 does not close until the plan has them. They go into the issue, every later step works against them, and QA ticks them off there.

The safety model

Contain. Curate. Control.

Docker's three layers for agent security (Docker), applied to a whole development workflow. Each is a separate answer to "what if the agent gets it wrong?"

C

Contain — the blast radius of a mistake

Every feature gets its own copy of the repository, its own dev server at its own address, and a database of its own. A migration written for one feature cannot reach another, and a database reset wipes nobody else's data.

C

Curate — what the agent can see and reach

Before the agent runs, SClaude sets its restrictions: only the context this stage needs, only the tools and permissions it needs, and the model this stage needs. The product describes itself in one config file, so nothing is assumed about anybody's repository.

C

Control — rules apply as the action happens

Hooks refuse forbidden commands at the moment a session tries them, by rules in layers. Tight by default, loosened by evidence: every refusal is logged and reviewed.

Quality gates

Done means proven, not said.

A model happily says "done" when it isn't, and a quick glance at its work misses what it skipped. So no step closes on its word:

  • A step closes on evidence — an approved plan, your yes and green tests, a real pull request, a review with nothing left open.
  • Tests run at up to four points: locally, on the preview, on production, and again after a rollback.
  • A review that cannot run is not waited on blindly — out of Claude quota or Actions minutes on GitHub, it is rerun after the reset, or carried on on this machine if the project says so.
  • Review is an agent's, the merge is a person's — Anthropic's own split (Anthropic, Claude Code Review). The decision arrives prepared: criteria as QA left them, checks, every finding and what was done about it.

When things go wrong

Every failure has a handled way out

Tests are red→retried once, then a fixing session, twice→still red? ask a person
Production is red→roll back first — unless someone shipped since→confirm with a clean check
QA sends it back→rewind to the step that must change→back through QA
The laptop closed→pick up where it stopped→nothing lost or redone

Every branch ends in something you can check, or a question to a person. Never in a loop, never in silence.

Retrospectives & gamification

Raw metrics bore people. Watching their own feature doesn't.

Every finished feature plays back on the process map: each step is highlighted in turn, with its minutes, your inputs and its cost. On the right is a real one. People watch it again and again, and start asking why it went the way it did.

  • What you may have missed — a warning that scrolled past, a promise a session did not keep.
  • How you could work better — where you waited, where you went round the same loop.
  • What went well — best practices coming from real features, with evidence, without a meeting.
  • What SClaude could do better — named down to the file, and fixed.

The rule: every point quotes a line from the recording or names a file. An empty section is a fine answer.

Coaching, not oversight

A private mirror, not a leaderboard.

The usual way — and why it fails

  • Token leaderboards — Meta took its board down (Engineering Leadership), Amazon closed its own after people burned tokens to climb it (The Next Web), Shopify renamed its board a usage dashboard (The Pragmatic Engineer).
  • AI champions made to mentor — as at Docusign (Docusign engineering blog): somebody else's duty, which rarely takes root.
  • Analytics for management — data collected about developers, for someone above them.
  • A senior's judgement from above — people defend themselves and hide from control.
vs

The SClaude way

  • You replay your own feature — not compared with others, not judged by another person.
  • Knowledge is shared without anyone noticing — through the tools everyone uses and improves.
  • Data is a by-product of a process that helps the developer move work forward.
  • Coaching one-to-one, from facts — from your own run, privately.

Metrics without overhead

Evidence, not status meetings.

Metrics usually cost developers time — fields in Jira, status reports, meetings — so they end up incomplete or invented. Here every feature leaves its record as it goes, and nobody fills anything in. The metrics page draws it:

  • DORA's four keys from real cycles, with rework, hotfix rate and lead time by stage.
  • The pull request's way — coding, pickup, review, merge and deploy time.
  • Quality — escaped defects, reopened issues, review threads, regressions green first time.
  • SClaude's own work — autonomy, the share of commits it wrote, and what a feature cost.
  • One radar — speed, stability, quality, requirements, flow, autonomy, cost, discipline.

Scale that stays flexible

A lesson climbs only as far as it proves itself.

If a process is too strict, people work around it. If it is too loose, it gives no data and no common ground. SClaude keeps one core process with freedom inside it: nothing changes it by accident, and everything that should change it can do so.

Away from the desk

Step away. The work keeps going.

Private by design

Your code, your services, your machine.

Data about your product goes only to you and to the services you already use.

No telemetry

Nothing goes to SClaude's authors: no usage statistics, no crash reports, no analytics. You choose in the settings whether to take updates and whether to send fixes back.

Your services, your logins

What it sends goes only where your work already goes: Anthropic, your GitHub, your deploy target, your own Telegram bot.

Data stays local

Transcripts, recordings, history and metrics stay on the machine, kept out of git and every deployment — unless you name a team repository for the metrics.

Nothing listens on the internet

Feature addresses answer only your machine and your private network; the phone's gate opens only while you turn it on.

Spec-driven development, with the 3Cs as the safety model.

Get started

Four lines to your first feature.

$ git clone git@github.com:personalautomation/SClaude.git ~/sclaude
$ cd ~/sclaude && ./install.sh
$ sclaude doctor          # what this machine still lacks, and how to fix it
$ cd <your product> && sclaude new

The first time you run sclaude new in a product, it creates the config for you. The guides — setting up, using it, for a team — are in the repository.
