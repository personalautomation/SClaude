Why it exists

Give a team an AI agent, and in a few months it is no longer one team.

A few people are ahead of the whole planet; others barely keep up. The leaders have no time to teach — a developer's job is to develop, not to mentor — and each of them spends about half their time, and their tokens, building the same private tooling, falling into holes a neighbour climbed out of long ago.

A zoo of bicycles, each just about coping with its owner's tasks.

And prompts are not guarantees. Even with every IMPORTANT in capitals, a model invents a status, switches language, skips a predefined option — and over time the process drifts.

SClaude is what came out of fixing both: the process moved into code, the tools moved into the company's git, and the model was left with what a model is actually better at — planning, writing and reviewing code.

€200a month on AI subscriptions for some developers — while others used a quarter of a €100 plan. Being ahead did not take a bigger plan; it took knowing how the model thinks.

What it solves

Five problems every team meets once AI writes the code.

Not a chat of agents watching each other. A harness that owns the process — so the work is safe, repeatable, shared, cheap and measured without anyone policing it.

1

Security — every stage gets exactly the tools it is allowed

  • A session per stage, each with its own permissions. Planning cannot write code; nothing can merge or push to main.
  • Rules are executable: hooks refuse a forbidden command the moment it is tried.
  • A mistake stays in one feature — its own copy, server, database and preview.
  • Tight by default, loosened by evidence: every refusal is logged and reviewed.
2

Scale and repeatability — one core, with room to bend it

  • One fixed path for every feature; a step closes on evidence, never on a model saying "done".
  • One copy for everybody. Nobody forks; a fix reaches the team at their next command.
  • Personal add-ons: the team and each developer layer their own rules and prompts.
  • The core is open to change: a lesson climbs from one developer to every product.
3

Shared knowledge instead of reinvented bicycles

  • One set of tools in the company's git, used and improved by everyone.
  • Process, prompts, rules and lessons compound instead of walking out of the door.
  • Knowledge flows without making anyone a mentor — and a new hire starts with everything the team has learned.
4

Fewer tokens — everything repeatable moved out of the agent

  • If a script can do it, the model should not. State, context, sandboxes, tests, deploys, rollbacks, the board — zero tokens.
  • A fresh session per step; only the approved plan is carried forward.
  • The right model per step — the strongest for planning, a cheaper one for the pull request, none for gates.
  • Context managed for you: warm prompt cache reused, noisy work sent to sub-agents, every pass priced.
5

Retrospectives that coach one-to-one, and metrics nobody fills in

  • A personal retrospective after every feature, from your own run: what you missed, how to work better, what went well. Facts, not opinions.
  • Not a lecture from above, not a leaderboard — a private mirror.
  • Gamified by accident: every feature plays back as an animation of its own path.
  • Metrics as a by-product: DORA, flow, quality, autonomy and cost — not one field in Jira.

Acceptance criteria are a gate, not a request. Step 1 does not close until the plan has them; they go into the issue, every later step works against them, and QA ticks them off there. Ctrl-C, a usage limit or a closed laptop closes nothing — the conversation resumes where it stopped.

The safety model

Contain. Curate. Control.

Docker's three layers for agent security, applied to a whole development workflow. Each is a separate answer to "what if the agent gets it wrong?"

C

Contain — the blast radius of a mistake

Every feature gets its own copy of the repository, its own dev server at its own address, and a database of its own. A migration written for one feature cannot reach another, and a database reset wipes nobody else's data.

C

Curate — what the agent can see and reach

The agent's world is built before it runs: the context for this stage and nothing stale, the tools and permissions it needs, the model it is worth. The product describes itself in one config file, so nothing is assumed about anybody's repository.

C

Control — rules apply as the action happens

Hooks refuse forbidden commands at the moment a session tries them. Rules come in layers — sclaude's core that nobody lifts, presets for the stack, the team's, the developer's — and every refusal is reviewed.

Merging a pull request — a person's decision
Pushing to main, force-pushing, skipping hooks
Promoting migrations, switching to production
Writing code during planning
Deploying to production without asking — and "anyway" when QA never saw it

These rules catch mistakes, not a session determined to get round them. Turn on branch protection for main as well, where your GitHub plan offers it.

Quality gates

Done means proven, not said.

  • A step closes on evidence — an approved plan, your yes and green tests, a real pull request, a review with nothing left open.
  • Tests run at up to four points: locally, on the preview, on production, and again after a rollback.
  • No endless loops. A red run is retried once, then a fixing session at most twice, then a person is asked.
  • Red production is rolled back first, and the old release checked — unless somebody else shipped since.
  • Review is an agent's, the merge is a person's. The decision arrives prepared: criteria as QA left them, checks, every finding and what was done about it.

Built for the bad days

Tests are red→a fixing session, twice→still red? ask a person
Production is red→roll back first→confirm with a clean check
QA sends it back→rewind to the step that must change→back through QA
The laptop closed→pick up where it stopped→nothing lost or redone

Every branch ends in something you can check, or a question to a person. Never in a loop, never in silence.

Retrospectives & gamification

Raw metrics bore people. Watching their own feature doesn't.

Every finished feature plays back on the process map — each step lit in turn, with its minutes, its prompts and its cost. People watch it again and again, and start asking why it went the way it did.

  • What you may have missed — a warning that scrolled past, a promise a session did not keep.
  • How you could work better — where you waited, where you went round the same loop.
  • What went well — best practices surfacing from real features, with evidence, without a meeting.
  • What sclaude could do better — named down to the file, and fixed.

The rule: every point quotes a line from the recording or names a file. An empty section is a fine answer.

How others do it

  • Token leaderboards — Meta took its board down, Amazon closed its own after people burned tokens to climb it, Shopify renamed its board a usage dashboard.
  • AI champions made to mentor — somebody else's duty, which rarely takes root.
  • Analytics for management — data collected about developers, for someone above them.
  • A senior's verdict from on high — people defend themselves and hide from oversight.

How SClaude does it

  • You replay your own feature — not compared with others, not judged by another person.
  • Knowledge is shared without anyone noticing — through the tools everyone uses and improves.
  • Data is a by-product of a process that helps the developer move work forward.
  • Coaching one-to-one, from facts — from your own run, privately.

Metrics without overhead

Evidence, not status meetings.

Not one field to fill in. Every feature leaves a record, and the metrics page draws it:

  • DORA's four keys from real cycles, with rework, hotfix rate and lead time by stage.
  • The pull request's way — coding, pickup, review, merge and deploy time.
  • Quality — escaped defects, reopened issues, review threads, regressions green first time.
  • Claude's own work — autonomy, the share of commits it wrote, and what a feature cost.
  • One radar — speed, stability, quality, requirements, flow, autonomy, cost, discipline.

Private by design

Your code, your services, your machine.

No telemetry

Nothing goes to SClaude's authors: no usage statistics, no crash reports, no analytics.

Your services, your logins

What it sends goes only where your work already goes: Anthropic, your GitHub, your deploy target, your own Telegram bot.

Data stays local

Transcripts, recordings, history and metrics stay on the machine, kept out of git and every deployment — unless you name a team repository for the metrics.

Nothing unasked

Updates and fixes sent back are each a setting you choose. A fix is checked for your product's details, and by default nothing leaves without your yes.

Nothing listens on the internet

Feature addresses answer only your machine and your private network; the phone's gate opens only while you turn it on.

Get started

Four lines to your first feature.

$ git clone git@github.com:personalautomation/SClaude.git ~/sclaude
$ cd ~/sclaude && ./install.sh
$ sclaude doctor          # what this machine still lacks, and how to fix it
$ cd <your product> && sclaude new

The first sclaude new in a product offers to write its config, read from what is already there. The guides — setting up, using it, for a team — are in the repository.
