Chapter 1 · The saddest part

The company became fragmented

The AI champions did not see why, or how, they should spend time sharing what they knew. They grew their own expertise and their own speed.

And that is normal
A developer's goal is to develop, not to mentor.

Somebody else's duties never take root.

So the idea came up
Besides developers, they needed a manager.

Not one who moves tickets. One who helps the team — who can read the team's tools as code, and run the team.

Chapter 1 · What I found first

Half their time went not into the product, but into tools

~50%

of the AI champions' time — and tokens — went into the tools they needed to automate how they develop features.

And that is normal.

What is not normal
  • Each of them, independently, built the same set of tools.
  • Each tripped over the holes their neighbour had solved long ago.
  • A zoo of bicycles, each just about coping with its owner's local tasks.
Chapter 1 · The first decision

All local tools go into the company's git. As is.

People resisted

"It's ugly in there"

They were ashamed to put their tools out.

→
The command

Put it out as is. Don't be shy.

→
Why it works

Everyone is ashamed of the same thing

So you can count on support, not shaming.

And it worked.

Chapter 1 · Two months later

Did developers start spending more time on the product? No.

Half the time still went into tools. But instead of reinventing bicycles, there were discussions, exchanged ideas, and questions to each other: why is it done slightly differently in your neighbour's tool?

Chapter 1 · Metrics

Metrics, without a single extra action from the developer

Somebody would say all of this can be reached by analysing metrics. I won't argue — but the main thing is that metrics must not add overhead to a developer's work.

  • Not a single field filled in Jira. No distracting developers from what they love most.
  • Metrics on the product, on token spend — even personal advice.
  • Collected while helping the developer move tasks forward and work with others.

If you can collect metrics that way — that is how it should be done.

Chapter 1 · Gamification

Raw metrics bore people. Watching their own feature get built doesn't.

Developers enjoyed watching how they built a feature, and played it back again and again. Along the way they started to think about why it went exactly like that.

▶ Watch

The whole feature as an animation: each step, how many prompts, how long the AI was waiting for them. Watching code being written is a thrill of vibe-coding; this is an even nicer sight.

↻ Replay

Again and again. People enjoy it after every finished feature.

? Think

Along the way (!), they talk about the process and where they could save.

Why did this feature go so strangely, and take so many steps?

Chapter 1 · Data

However much we talk about AI, it is built on your data

With the speed of AI development, its best practices are hard to follow: any book is outdated as soon as it is published.

No data — no improvement. Let alone AI agents and helpers.

And we always hit the same thing: how far the team follows the core process, and whether the data is complete and honest.

A process comfortable for the team, plus a feedback loop

1 · Change the process quickly

2 · Room for personalisation

Developers may have their own add-ons on top of the core process. It shouldn't be too tight.

Follow the core process, with freedom inside it — you have metrics.
Don't — and there is no data.
Chapter 1 · How others do it

I went another way, and I am proud of it

OthersMy way
Gamification Token leaderboards. Meta took its "Claudeonomics" board down, Amazon closed its own after people burned tokens to climb it, Shopify renamed its board a usage dashboard. You replay your own feature. It is not comparing yourself with others — you think about yourself, let alone being judged by another person.
Knowledge AI champions are made to mentor: Docusign's Eng AI Champions, and engineers embedded in teams as peer mentors. Mentoring is somebody else's duty. The developer is not distracted, and knowledge is shared without anyone noticing.
Data Analytics for management: DX, Jellyfish, LinearB, the usage leaderboard in Claude Code's own analytics. Data is a by-product of a process that helps the developer.
